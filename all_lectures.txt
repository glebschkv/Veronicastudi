                                     Glossary of Key Terms
Intelligence
A general mental capability involving reasoning, planning, problem-solving, abstract thinking, understanding
complex ideas, learning quickly, and learning from experience. Often linked to the ability to adapt effectively to
one’s environment.

General mental ability
The broad, overarching capacity that underlies performance across many different cognitive tasks (sometimes
referred to as “g” in intelligence research).

Capacity to learn from experience
The aspect of intelligence that reflects how well a person can learn, update, and use knowledge based on past
events.

Capacity to adapt to the environment
The ability to change behaviour, strategies or thinking in response to demands or challenges in one’s surroundings.

Hypothetical construct
An explanatory concept used in psychology that cannot be observed directly (e.g., intelligence, motivation,
aggression) but is inferred from behaviour or performance.
Operationalisation
The process of turning a fuzzy, abstract concept (like “intelligence” or “aggression”) into something that can be
measured concretely (e.g., IQ test scores, number of verbal attacks).

Construct
A theoretical idea or concept (such as intelligence, overconfidence, wisdom) that researchers define and measure
using operationalisations.

Implicit theory of intelligence
A person’s everyday, informal beliefs about what intelligence is and what an intelligent person is like. These are not
necessarily based on formal data and are often shaped by culture, age, and personal experience.

Explicit theory of intelligence
A formal, scientific theory of intelligence developed by researchers, based on systematic data (e.g., test
performance, factor analysis).

Layperson (or lay conception)
A non-expert member of the public; in this context, someone without specialist training who still holds
beliefs/theories about intelligence.

Expert (in intelligence research)
A psychologist or researcher who studies intelligence professionally and develops explicit, data-driven theories.
Practical problem-solving ability
A facet of intelligence referring to the ability to reason logically about everyday problems, especially when solutions
are not obvious and situations are complex.

Verbal ability
The capacity to use language effectively – including vocabulary, verbal fluency, clear expression, and understanding
spoken or written language.

Social competence
Skills that allow individuals to function effectively in social situations, including understanding others, being accepted
by others, and navigating social relationships.

Academic intelligence
A form of intelligence associated with success in educational settings – often connected to book learning, exams, and
formal academic tasks.

Everyday intelligence
Intelligence as reflected in daily life – managing practical problems, relationships, and real-world situations, not just
academic tasks.

Unintelligence
Behaviours or characteristics people associate with a lack of intelligence (e.g., poor decision-making, not learning
from mistakes).
Cultural conceptions of intelligence
The ways different cultures define and value intelligence (e.g., Western emphasis on speed and efficiency vs
Eastern inclusion of social, historical, and spiritual dimensions).

Western conceptions of intelligence
Implicit theories common in Western cultures that emphasise quick mental processing, efficiency, problem-solving
speed, and clear verbal articulation.

Eastern (Non-Western) conceptions of intelligence
Implicit theories that include not only cognitive skills but also social harmony, historical knowledge, and spiritual
considerations when solving problems.

Everyday competence
The ability to handle daily tasks and challenges effectively in real-life contexts (e.g., managing finances, dealing with
practical problems).

Ability to deal with novelty
How well someone can handle new, unfamiliar situations – a component often associated with intelligence,
especially in younger adults.

Life-span perspective on intelligence
The idea that conceptions of what “intelligent behaviour” looks like differ depending on age (e.g., babies vs children
vs adults vs older adults).

                                       Glossary of Key Terms
Intelligence
A broad mental ability that helps us learn, reason, solve problems and adapt to new situations. Hard to define
exactly; often treated as “general mental ability”.

Mental abilities
Specific things the mind can do, such as remembering, reasoning, using language, solving problems, or
manipulating shapes in space.

Operationalisation / Operational definition
Turning a fuzzy idea (like “intelligence” or “aggression”) into something we can actually measure (e.g. score on a
test, number of shoves, answers to certain questions).

General intelligence (g, g-factor)
A statistical factor that captures what is common across lots of different mental tests. If you tend to do well on
many different types of tests, you are said to have higher g.

Specific intelligences
Abilities that are more narrow or specialised (e.g. verbal, spatial, mathematical, mechanical). You can be stronger
in one area than another.
Savant syndrome
A condition where someone demonstrates exceptional aptitude in one domain, such as art or mathematics, with
such aptitude often coinciding with some form of social or intellectual impairment.

IQ (Intelligence Quotient)
A single score meant to summarise someone’s performance on an ‘intelligence test’ compared to the general
population. Modern IQ scores are based on how far above or below the average (100) a person scores.

Mental age
In early IQ tests: the age level at which a child is performing. For example, if a 7-year-old passes most items
typical of 9-year-olds, we might say they have a mental age of 9.

Chronological / physical age
Your actual age in years (e.g. 10 years old), used to compare with mental age in early IQ formulas.

IQ ratio (Stern’s formula)
An early way of calculating IQ in children:
IQ = Mental age / Physical age × 100
e.g. mental age 8 / physical age 10 × 100 = IQ 80.

Norm-referenced / relative measure
Scores are meaningful only compared to a large group (the “norm” sample) of people of the same age (or other
criterion). IQ is defined relative to others, not as an absolute quantity.
Ordinal measure
A scale that tells you the order (who scored higher or lower), but not “how much” more ability someone has. IQ is
treated as ordinal: 120 is higher than 100, but not “20% more intelligence”.

Independent criterion (of intelligence)
An external measure that we treat as a standard to judge a test against—for example, school grades or job
performance. Good intelligence tests should correlate with such criteria.

Consequential behaviour
Real-world outcomes we care about, such as school success or job performance, that we might want intelligence
tests to predict.

Standardised test
A test that is given and scored in the same way for everyone, under the same conditions, so scores can be
compared fairly across people.

Normal distribution / “bell curve”
A common pattern of scores where most people are in the middle, and fewer people are very high or very low. IQ
scores in a large sample tend to form a bell-shaped curve.

Mean
The average score. For IQ, by definition, the mean is set to 100.
Standard deviation (SD)
A measure of how spread out the scores are around the mean. For IQ, SD = 15. Being 1 SD above the mean is IQ
115; 2 SD above is IQ 130, etc.

Percentile
Shows the percentage of people who score below you. Being in the top 2.5% means you scored higher than about
97.5% of people your age.

Average IQ
Usually defined as IQ 85–115 (about 68% of people fall in this range).

Gifted (in IQ terms)
Defined here as IQ above about 145; roughly less than the top 1% of the population, depending on the cut-off
used.

Intellectual disability (in IQ terms)
Traditionally defined as IQ below about 70 (~2.5%), usually combined with difficulties in everyday functioning. Note
IQ score alone not enough to define this term in clinical/educational settings.
Historical figures & tests
Sir Francis Galton (1822–1911)
Early pioneer of measuring individual differences; founded differential psychology and helped start
psychometrics. Tried to measure intelligence via basic sensory and reaction time tasks. Coined “nature vs
nurture”.

Differential psychology
The branch of psychology that studies how and why individuals differ from one another in traits such as
intelligence, personality, etc.

Psychometrics
The science of psychological measurement—designing, testing and analysing questionnaires and tests (e.g. IQ
tests) to ensure they are reliable and valid.

Anthropometric Laboratory
Galton’s London lab where he collected large amounts of data (around 9,000 people) on physical and sensory
measures, hoping to relate them to mental ability.

James McKeen Cattell (1860–1944)
Psychologist who, following Galton, developed the first set of “mental tests” (e.g. reaction time, weight
discrimination) to measure individual differences.
Clark Wissler (1870–1947)
Used correlation statistics to show Cattell’s mental tests did not predict college grades (and didn’t even correlate
with each other), which undermined that early approach to IQ.

Alfred Binet (1857–1911)
Commissioned by the French government to develop the first practical intelligence test for children (the Binet–
Simon scale) to identify those needing extra support. Focused on everyday tasks and age-appropriate items.

Binet–Simon scale
Early intelligence test with sets of tasks grouped by age (e.g. 4-year-old tasks, 6-year-old tasks). The pattern of
items a child could pass was used to estimate their mental age.

Lewis Terman (1877–1956)
American psychologist who revised Binet’s test into the Stanford–Binet test, expanded it, and helped popularise
IQ testing in the US.

Stanford–Binet test
A major revision of the Binet–Simon scale produced at Stanford University; became one of the standard IQ tests
and is still used today (in updated forms).

William Stern (1871–1938)
Psychologist who introduced the term “Intelligence Quotient” (IQ) and proposed the mental-age/chronological-age
ratio formula for children’s IQ.
Robert Yerkes (1876–1956)
Psychologist who, with colleagues, developed large-scale IQ tests (Army Alpha and Beta) for the US Army
during World War I, boosting the popularity of mental testing.

Army Alpha and Beta tests
Group intelligence tests used by the US Army: Alpha for literate recruits, Beta for illiterate or non-English
speakers. Multiple-choice, timed tasks given to huge numbers of people.

                                 Glossary of Key Terms
Wechsler tests and related terms
•Wechsler Intelligence Tests
A family of widely used, individually administered IQ tests (for adults and children) that use many different subtests
to profile intelligence.

•WAIS (Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale)
The main Wechsler test for adults and older adolescents (approx. 16–90 years). Gives an overall IQ plus index
scores (e.g. Verbal Comprehension, Working Memory, Perceptual Organisation, Processing Speed).

•WISC (Wechsler Intelligence Scale for Children)
The Wechsler test designed specifically for school-age children (roughly 5–16 years).

•Subtest
One of the individual tasks within a larger IQ test battery (e.g. Vocabulary, Block Design). Each subtest targets a
particular aspect of cognitive ability.

•Verbal Comprehension
WAIS index capturing understanding and use of language (e.g. defining words, explaining proverbs, answering
general-knowledge questions).
•Perceptual Reasoning
WAIS index measuring non-verbal reasoning and visual–spatial problem solving (e.g. Block Design, Matrix
Reasoning, Visual Puzzles).

•Working Memory
WAIS index measuring the ability to temporarily hold and manipulate information in mind (e.g. Digit Span,
Arithmetic, Letter–Number Sequencing).

•Processing Speed
WAIS index measuring how quickly and accurately simple information can be processed (e.g. Coding, Symbol
Search, Cancellation).

•Deviation IQ
IQ defined in terms of how far a person’s score deviates from the average score for their age group (mean = 100,
SD = 15), rather than using mental-age/chronological-age ratios.

•Standardisation / Norming
The process of giving a test to a large, representative sample (varying in age, sex, social class, region, etc.) to
create norms that individual scores can be compared against.
Raven’s Progressive Matrices

•Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPM)
A non-verbal IQ test where people identify the missing piece in a pattern of shapes. Designed to measure
abstract reasoning and to be relatively independent of language and culture.

•Abstract reasoning
The ability to detect patterns, relationships and rules between shapes, ideas, or pieces of information, beyond
concrete, everyday content.

•Eductive ability
Raven’s term for the ability to “draw out” meaning from complex or confusing information and see underlying
patterns.

•Reproductive ability
The ability to store, recall, and reproduce learned information.
Correlations, reliability and validity
•Correlation
A statistical measure (ranging from −1 to +1) describing how strongly two variables move together. Positive
correlations between subtests mean people who score high on one tend to score high on others.

•Hierarchy of mental abilities
The idea that test scores can be organised in levels: individual subtests → clusters or “index scores” (e.g. Verbal
Comprehension) → a general factor “g” at the top.

•Reliability
How consistently a test measures something.

•Internal reliability
The extent to which items within a test or subtest correlate with each other, suggesting they measure the same
underlying construct.

•Test–retest reliability
The extent to which a test gives similar results when the same person takes it on different occasions (assuming
no real change in their ability).
•Validity
Whether a test actually measures what it claims to measure (here: intelligence), and whether it predicts relevant
outcomes.

•Face validity
The extent to which a test appears, on the surface, to measure what it claims to (e.g. an IQ test that “looks like”
it measures reasoning and problem-solving).

•Concurrent validity
When a test correlates well with other established measures of the same construct (e.g. WAIS scores correlating
with other IQ tests).

•Predictive validity
The degree to which test scores forecast future outcomes such as school achievement, job performance, or
training success.
Uses and misuses of IQ tests
•Educational use
Using IQ tests to help identify children who may need extra support or who are exceptionally able, to plan
appropriate educational provision.

•Clinical / diagnostic use
Using IQ tests as part of an assessment for intellectual disability, learning difficulties, or the cognitive impact of
brain injury, dementia, etc.

•Occupational use
Using cognitive ability tests as one component in selection, placement, or training decisions in workplaces.

•Cultural bias
The criticism that some IQ tests are more familiar or appropriate for people from certain cultural or educational
backgrounds, which can disadvantage others.

•Eugenics
A movement (especially in the early 20th century) that misused IQ tests to justify policies like forced sterilisation,
immigration restriction, and racial discrimination.

Validity of intelligence tests
Validity refers to the question of whether the test measures what it claims to
measure.
 Face validity
An IQ test is said to have face validity if it "looks like" it is going to measure what
it is supposed to measure.
 Concurrent validity
Intelligence test shows concurrent validity if it shows a (positive) relationship
with other measures of intelligence.
 Predictive validity
Intelligence tests have been used to predict ‘real-world‘ measures of
intelligence or achievement (e.g. school achievement, job performance, etc.)
Validity of intelligence tests
Reliability refers to two aspects:
Internal reliability
Any measure of intelligence with good internal reliability will have a
number of items that correlate positively with one other, this
suggests they are measures of the same construct.
Test-retest reliability
A good intelligence test will show a good level of reliability over time.
Your general intelligence is thought to be relatively stable over time.
You would expect IQ scores to be very similar across test sessions.

                                 Glossary of Key Terms
Factor Analysis
A statistical technique used to identify clusters of related variables. It reveals underlying “latent factors” (e.g.,
intelligence, personality traits or depression) that explain correlations among tests.

Correlation
A measure of how strongly two variables relate. Positive correlations underpin the discovery of g and broader
mental ability groupings.

g (General Intelligence)
The general mental ability underlying performance across all cognitive tasks. Proposed by Spearman to explain
why test scores are positively correlated.

Specific Abilities (s)
Task-specific skills unique to particular tests (e.g., memory for digits, spatial rotation). In Spearman’s model,
each test reflects g + a specific factor.

Spearman’s Two-Factor Theory
Intelligence consists of:
g — a general ability shared by all tests, and
s — abilities unique to each task.
                                   Glossary of Key Terms
Primary Mental Abilities (Thurstone)
Seven broad, separable abilities (e.g., verbal comprehension, spatial, numerical). A challenge to Spearman, though
still positively correlated.

Positive Manifold
The consistent finding that all cognitive tests positively correlate with each other. The main empirical basis for g.

Three-Stratum Model (Carroll)
A hierarchical model summarising decades of factor-analytic data:
•Stratum III: g
•Stratum II: broad abilities (e.g., memory, spatial, verbal) - cognitive domains
•Stratum I: narrow, task-specific abilities – cognitive tasks

Fluid Intelligence (gf)
Ability to solve novel problems, reason abstractly, and detect patterns. Less dependent on prior knowledge;
measured well by Raven’s Progressive Matrices.

Crystallized Intelligence (gc)
Knowledge-based skills accumulated through experience and education (e.g., vocabulary, general knowledge).
Increases across adulthood.
                                  Glossary of Key Terms
Psychometric Approach
Studying intelligence through test scores and their statistical relationships. Focus: What patterns are in the data?

Cognitive Approach
Examines the mental processes and biological mechanisms underlying performance. Focus: How does the mind
solve tasks?

Elementary Cognitive Tasks (ECTs)
Simple tasks measuring speed and efficiency of basic processes (e.g., reaction time, inspection time). Moderately
correlated with g.

Reaction Time (RT)
Speed of responding to a stimulus. Lower RT and lower variability tend to correlate with higher intelligence.

Inspection Time (IT)
The minimum time needed to discriminate between stimuli. Shorter IT is associated with higher gf.

Multiple Intelligences (Gardner)
A non-psychometric theory proposing several independent intelligences (e.g., linguistic, musical, spatial,
interpersonal). Based on neuropsychology, prodigies, cultural evidence—not factor analysis.
                                   Glossary of Key Terms
Latent Variable
An unobserved construct inferred from measurable indicators, such as g being inferred from correlations across
tests.

Eigenvalue
A statistic in factor analysis indicating how much variance a factor explains. Factors with eigenvalues >1 are often
retained.

Communality
The proportion of variance in each variable explained by the retained factors.

Rotated Component Matrix
A table showing how strongly each variable loads on each factor after rotation, used to interpret and label the
factors.

Symbol Systems (Gardner)
Representational tools associated with each intelligence (language, maps, musical notation), used as part of
Gardner’s evidence for modularity.

Neuropsychological Dissociation
Cases where one cognitive ability is impaired while another is preserved, used to argue for separability of abilities.

                 Multiple Intelligences Approach
                        • Not psychometric and not cognitive in the traditional sense.

                        • Gardner argued that traditional IQ tests measure only a narrow slice of human
                          abilities (mainly linguistic and logical-mathematical)

                        • Proposed that intelligence is plural, not singular — humans possess multiple
                          independent intelligences, each with its own developmental trajectory and
                          neural basis.
Howard Gardner
                        • Emphasis on real-world functioning, cultural value, and adaptive usefulness of
                          different skills.

                        • Intelligences are partly modular, meaning that damage to one brain system
                          may impair one ability but leave others intact (e.g., musical talent preserved
                          despite language deficits).

     Gardner rejected the idea of a single “g” factor, arguing instead that humans have several relatively
  independent intelligences, each rooted in different brain systems and serving different adaptive functions.
                     Multiple Intelligences Approach
Gardner’s theory grew from the following influences:
Neuropsychological dissociations
Selective brain damage can impair one ability (e.g., language) while sparing others (e.g., music, spatial skills)

Prodigies & Savants
Exceptional strengths in a single domain alongside profound weaknesses elsewhere show that abilities can develop
independently.

Developmental trajectories:
Children show uneven skill growth (e.g., early musical talent but average reasoning), implying domain-specific
development.

Cross-cultural observations:
Different societies value different adaptive skills (navigation, storytelling, social leadership), indicating multiple forms of
competence.

Evolutionary considerations:
Proposed intelligences map onto capacities that likely offered survival advantages (e.g., spatial, interpersonal, naturalistic).

Symbol systems:
Each intelligence uses its own representational system (musical notation, language, maps, gestures), consistent with
distinct cognitive domains.

    Does Empirical Evidence Support the MI Theory?
                          There is evidence for:

                          • Neuropsychological dissociations (e.g., amusia vs preserved language, spatial
                            impairments but intact social intelligence).

                          • Domain-specific learning systems in cognitive science (e.g., numerical cognition, language
                            acquisition, face processing).

                          • Individual differences profiles, where people genuinely show strengths in one domain but
  Howard Gardner            not others.

Major Criticisms / Limited Evidence

• Basis of Gardner’s model is not empirical. Like Freud, he provides more of a literary analysis of insight. Despite
  conceptual appeal, MI lacks strong factor-analytic / psychometric or other quantitative approaches.

• Gardner’s intelligences are not statistically independent: his proposed intelligences tend to correlate with each other.
  Factor analyses consistently produce a large g factor, plus a few broad group factors (verbal, spatial, memory, etc.)

• However, very popular theory beyond academia > appeals to ideal we all have some special ability, > doesn’t
  recognize limitations implied by g, > based on anecdotes / observations easy to understand

•   Factor analysis - statistical technique to represent correlations between intelligence tests, which can be
    clustered into more specific factors, e.g. reasoning, vocabulary etc.

•   Correlations between all factors are positive (a highly replicated result) – implying that all the factors
    derived from individual tests have something in common - called the g-factor or g

•   Spearman’s 2-factor theory of intelligence: specific abilities (‘s‘) and general ability (‘g‘)

•   Thurstone, suggested that there were 7 separable human mental abilities.

•   John Carroll’s ‘three stratum model’ of human cognitive ability allows for individual variability in cognitive
    domains between individuals with similar IQ scores

•   Cattell subdivided g into Fluid and Crystallized intelligence

•   Useful to consider cognitive processes as well as statistical patterns in IQ scores

•   Reaction times / processing speed fairly well correlated with g

•   Gardner’s popular model proposes multiple intelligences but borne out of observation more than

                               Glossary of Key Terms
Brain Structure Terms

Grey Matter / Cortical Thickness
Thickness of the brain’s outer layer containing neuron cell bodies. Thicker cortex correlates positively
with g and IQ.

White Matter Integrity
Quality of axonal connections ("wires") enabling communication between regions. Higher integrity →
higher g.

White Matter Hyperintensities / “Scars”
Age-related lesions interrupting processing efficiency. Higher WMH → lower intelligence.

Total Brain Volume
Volume inside skull minus cerebrospinal fluid. Meta-analytic correlations with intelligence ≈ .28–.31.
                                Glossary of Key Terms
Fluid Intelligence (gf)
Ability to solve novel problems, reason abstractly, and manage interference (e.g., Raven’s
Matrices). Linked strongly to working memory and PFC activity.

Crystallised Intelligence (gc)
Knowledge accumulated through experience (e.g., vocabulary, facts). Distinct from gf.

MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging)
Measures brain structure (grey matter thickness, white matter integrity, total brain volume).

fMRI (Functional MRI)
Measures blood flow changes during cognitive tasks. Used in Gray et al. (2003) to link working
memory load to PFC activity.

EEG (Electroencephalography)
Measures electrical activity at rest or in response to stimuli.

MEG (Magnetoencephalography)
Detects magnetic fields created by neural electrical activity.
                                 Glossary of Key Terms
Neuron / Brain Cell
Basic information-processing units of the brain.

Dendrites / Dendritic Complexity
Branch-like structures that collect inputs. Larger/more complex dendritic trees predict higher IQ.

Action Potential (AP)
Electrical signal fired by a neuron. Faster APs were observed in individuals with higher IQ.

Pyramidal Cells
Major excitatory neurons in the cortex; crucial for advanced cognition. Differences in dendritic size and AP
speed correlate with intelligence.

Prefrontal Cortex (PFC)
Region linked to working memory, reasoning, interference control. Left lateral PFC activation correlates strongly
with fluid intelligence.

Temporal Cortex
Region where cortical thickness correlates with IQ and where neuron samples were taken in Goriounova et al.
(2018).
Working Memory (WM)
Short-term storage and manipulation of information. Strongly associated with gf. Assessed via the “3-back” task
with lures.

n-Back Task
A cognitive task requiring recall of stimuli presented “n” steps earlier. Used to tax working memory/interference
control.

Lure Trials
High-interference trials in the n-back task. Accuracy on lure trials correlates strongly with gf.

Connectome
Map of all neural connections in the brain.

Morphometric Similarity Networks (MSNs)
MRI-derived measures showing how similar brain regions are in structure, used to estimate connectivity.

Nodal Degree / Hubness
Number of connections a brain region has. Higher degree (“more hub-like”) regions in frontal and temporal cortex
predict higher IQ.

--- Slide 3 ---
Glossary of Key Terms
IQ (Intelligence Quotient)
A standardised score (mean = 100, SD = 15) used to represent general cognitive ability. IQ scores can be compared across individuals and groups.
Standard Deviation (SD)
A measure of variability. In IQ, 1 SD = 15 points. Sex-difference studies often look at whether boys' and girls' IQ scores show different variability (spread).
Variance / Variability
How spread out the scores are in a population. In this lecture, a key finding is greater male variability, meaning more boys appear at both high and low extremes of IQ.
Scottish Mental Surveys (1932)
A near-whole-population test of 11-year-olds (≈40k girls; ≈40k boys). Found no mean sex difference in intelligence, but differences in score spread. Boys more common at the extremes.
National Longitudinal Survey of Youth (NLSY)
A large US dataset with opposite-sex siblings. Found a tiny male advantage in general intelligence (d = .064), too small to be meaningful. Variability differences again appeared.

--- Slide 4 ---
Glossary of Key Terms
Armed Forces Qualification Test (AFQT)
A multi-subtest cognitive battery used in NLSY. Produces a general intelligence score.
Cohen’s d
A measure of effect size showing how big a group difference is.
0.2 = small
0.5 = medium
0.8 = large
Cohen’s (1988) rules of thumb
Most male–female intelligence differences fall in the very small range.
Effect Size
A statistic representing the magnitude of a difference, independent of sample size.

Glossary of Key Terms
Raven’s Progressive Matrices (RPM)
A non-verbal reasoning test often considered a relatively “pure” measure of g. Meta-analyses claim a small male advantage in adults, but findings are inconsistent and influenced by visuospatial components.
Visuospatial Reasoning
The ability to mentally manipulate and visualise objects in space (e.g., mental rotation, spatial perception). Shows the largest male advantage among cognitive tests.
Mental Rotation
A visuospatial task requiring participants to imagine objects rotating in space. Shows a medium–large male advantage across ages.
Spatial Visualisation / Spatial Perception
Other spatial skills involving arranging, analysing, or interpreting spatial relations. Typically favour males, but effects are small to moderate.
Verbal Ability
Cognitive skills involving comprehension, vocabulary, reading and writing. Women show small advantages in verbal fluency, comprehension and especially writing performance (large effect in NAEP data).

Glossary of Key Terms
Stereotype Threat
A situational phenomenon where individuals perform worse when reminded of a negative stereotype about their group (e.g., women and maths tests). Johns et al use a between-groups design showing how instructions referencing stereotypes can impair performance.
Biological Explanations (e.g., Testosterone Levels)
Example: Women with PCOS (higher testosterone) perform worse on tasks where females typically have an advantage (verbal memory, fluency). Supports possible hormonal influences, though effects are selective.
Between-Groups Design
An experimental design comparing two separate groups (e.g., stereotype vs no-stereotype condition). Used to assess effects such as stereotype threat.
Stanine Scores
A type of standardised score used in CAT (1–9 scale). Useful for comparing boy/girl distributions across score bands.
Extreme Scores
Refers to individuals falling at the very low or very high ends of the cognitive distribution. Multiple datasets show more males at both extremes even when means are equal.

                        Glossary of Key Terms
Genes
Segments of DNA that code for proteins and influence physical and psychological traits, including aspects of
intelligence.

Proteins
Molecules produced from gene instructions that carry out most biological functions, including those in the
brain.

Cognitive Power
A general term for mental abilities such as reasoning, memory, problem-solving, learning, and processing
speed.

Heritability
The proportion of differences between individuals in a trait (e.g., intelligence) that can be explained by genetic
variation within a population.

Shared Environment
Environmental influences that people raised together tend to share, such as household experiences,
socioeconomic conditions, or parenting style.
Non-shared Environment
Environmental factors unique to each individual - experiences, peers, illnesses, teachers - making people less
similar to their siblings.

Monozygotic (MZ) Twins
Identical twins who share 100% of their genes; used to estimate genetic influences on traits.

Dizygotic (DZ) Twins
Non-identical twins who share roughly 50% of their genes; used as a comparison group in behavioural
genetics.

Twin Studies
A major method for estimating the contributions of genes, shared environment, and non-shared environment by
comparing similarities between MZ and DZ twins.

Family Studies
Research exploring how traits run in biological families to estimate genetic influence.

Adoption Studies
Studies comparing adopted children to their biological relatives (genetic influence) and adoptive families
(environmental influence).
Correlation Coefficient
A numerical estimate of similarity between individuals on a trait. Higher correlations for MZ than DZ twins suggest
genetic influence.

Genome-Wide Association Study (GWAS)
A genetic method scanning the whole genome to identify variants associated with traits such as intelligence.

Single Nucleotide Polymorphism (SNP)
A single DNA base variation. Individuals differ at millions of these positions, making them useful markers in
GWAS.

General Intelligence (g)
A statistical construct reflecting the shared variance across different cognitive tasks; commonly used in genetic
and psychological research.

Gene × Environment Interaction
The concept that genes and environments do not act separately but influence each other - environmental
experiences can activate or modify genetic tendencies.

Genetic Contribution
The proportion of variation in intelligence explained by genetic differences between people.
Shared Environmental Contribution
The proportion of variation explained by factors siblings share, typically decreasing as people get older.

Non-shared Environmental Contribution
The portion of variation explained by unique individual experiences, relatively stable across development.

Stability of Intelligence
The observation that intelligence remains relatively consistent throughout adulthood, even though small changes
can occur across the lifespan.

Flynn Effect
The sustained increase in average IQ scores across generations, particularly pronounced for non-verbal (fluid)
intelligence tests.

                          Glossary of Key Terms
Giftedness
Exceptionally high ability or potential relative to age peers, often identified statistically (e.g. IQ ≈ 130+), but
increasingly defined using multiple attributes (intellectual ability, creativity, achievement, motivation, leadership).

Child prodigy
A child (typically under age 10) who performs at an adult professional level in a demanding, culturally recognised
domain (e.g. music, chess, maths), defined by realised achievement rather than potential.

Precocity
Rapid early mastery of knowledge or skills within a domain; often preferred to prodigy because it avoids mythic or
“unnatural” connotations.

IQ (Intelligence Quotient)
A standardised measure of general cognitive ability; prodigies typically have above-average IQ, but extreme IQ is
neither necessary nor sufficient.

Domain specificity
The principle that exceptional performance is usually confined to one domain (e.g. music, chess), rather than
reflecting global superiority.
                         Glossary of Key Terms
Above-level testing
Assessment using tests designed for older individuals (e.g. SAT taken in early adolescence) to differentiate ability
within the gifted range (used extensively in SMPY).

Working memory
The capacity to hold and manipulate information over short periods; consistently identified as a key strength in
prodigies across multiple domains.

Rage to learn
An intense, sustained drive to acquire knowledge or mastery; a motivational feature often highlighted in extreme
cases of prodigious achievement.

Co-incidence framework (Feldman)
A theoretical model proposing that prodigies emerge from a rare alignment of natural talent, family and
teacher support, domain readiness, and historical–cultural context.

Sensitive periods
Developmental windows of heightened receptivity to learning; in prodigies, these may become unusually intense
and individualised.

Measurement of practice/study time is limited

•   Howard estimated chess study using a small number of self-report questions in an online survey. Critics argue

      Child Prodigies - Theoretical Interpretations
Persistent definitional problems:
The term prodigy carries historical and cultural connotations of being “unnatural,”
leading many scholars to prefer precocity, which emphasises rapid early mastery
without invoking mystique.

Limits of IQ-based explanations:
Prodigies usually show above-average intelligence, but extreme IQ is neither
necessary nor sufficient; exceptional domain-specific performance cannot be
explained by general intelligence alone.

Domain specificity and structure:
Prodigies emerge primarily in highly structured domains (e.g. music, chess, maths),
where progress and excellence can be clearly defined and measured.
      Child Prodigies - Theoretical Interpretations
Feldman’s co-incidence framework:
Prodigy arises from a rare alignment of natural endowment, intensive family and
teacher support, a developmentally receptive domain, and a supportive historical
and cultural context.

Developmental and motivational factors:
Theories emphasise distinctive cognitive and emotional features, including intense,
sustained motivation (“rage to learn”), beyond what IQ tests capture.

Sensitive periods reinterpreted:
Prodigies may experience individualised sensitive periods, where early interests
become unusually deep and stable, underpinning long-term commitment and
expertise.
                Overall Summary
• Lecture distinguishes giftedness (high potential) from child prodigy
  (early professional-level achievement in a specific domain). While all
  prodigies are gifted, most gifted children are not prodigies.


• Evidence from major longitudinal studies (e.g. Terman, SMPY) shows
  that early cognitive ability predicts later educational and occupational
  outcomes, even among very high-ability individuals. However, ability
  alone does not determine life success; interests, motivation, and
  opportunity play crucial roles.
                Overall Summary
• Research on child prodigies demonstrates that prodigious
  performance is domain-specific, develops over time, and does not
  require extreme IQ. Instead, prodigies typically show above-average
  intelligence, exceptionally strong working memory, intense
  motivation, and sustained support from families and expert teachers.


• Prodigies cannot be explained by a single factor such as IQ or
  practice. Instead, they emerge from a rare alignment of cognitive
  abilities, motivational characteristics, environmental support, and
  cultural–historical context.

           What IQ Tests Actually Measure
• Most IQ tests (e.g. WAIS, Raven’s, etc.) are designed to measure:

     > General cognitive ability (g) – shared variance across
     tasks.
     > Subdomains: e.g. verbal comprehension, working memory,
     processing speed, visuospatial reasoning.

• They use structured, often time-limited, decontextualised tasks
  under standardised conditions.

Cultural context and test bias

• IQ tests have been criticised as culturally
  loaded and reflecting Western, schooled
  definitions of intelligence.

• Abilities valued in some cultures (e.g. social
  responsibility, community wisdom, ecological
  knowledge) may not show up in IQ test
  scores.

• Sternberg’s cross-cultural work: children
  may perform poorly on Western-style tests
  but show high levels of practical intelligence
  in everyday tasks in their own context.
                       What intelligence tests omit
• Personality & Motivation
• IQ is only part of the story for academic and life outcomes.

• Personality traits, especially conscientiousness (organisation, diligence, self-discipline), are
  robust predictors of school and university performance (alongside intelligence), e.g. Dumfart &
  Neubauer, 2016

• Meta-analytic and large-scale studies:
       >Conscientiousness often rivals or exceeds IQ in predicting grades, particularly when
       content is less abstract.
       >Other traits (e.g. openness to experience) also matter, but effects are smaller or depend
       on domain, e.g. Verbree et al. 2021
       >Broader non-cognitive factors: Grit, self-efficacy, time management, and low test anxiety
       predict GPA, especially in diverse and nontraditional student populations, e.g. Cho &
       Serrano 2020

• IQ tests do not assess these characteristics.
